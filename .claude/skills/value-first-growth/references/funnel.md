# The funnel, stage by stage

Seven stages. Each has a job, a failure mode, and a test you can actually run.

---

## 1. First open → first proof

**Job:** show the customer one true thing about their own life, in under two minutes.

Day-one retention in health apps is 20–30%. Seven or eight of every ten people who open the
app are never coming back. Everything downstream is competing for what happens in that one
session, and trial starts peak on day 0 across every model measured. There is no later.

The move is a **cost mirror**: ask four or five short questions, then reflect the answer
back as a number the customer has never seen stated plainly. Not a promise about the
future — an arithmetic fact about the present, computed from what they just typed.

> You said about three hours a day. That's 21 hours a week. Around 45 days a year.

This works because it is true, because they supplied the inputs, and because nobody has
ever done the multiplication for them. It creates motivation without claiming an outcome,
which means it survives contact with a regulator, an app store reviewer, and a clinician.

**Failure mode:** a welcome carousel. Three screens of feature marketing before anything is
computed. It is the default and it is why most onboarding completion sits at 30–50%.

**Test:** can a stranger open the app and see a number derived from their own answers before
they get bored? Time it with a stopwatch, not an estimate.

---

## 2. First proof → commitment

**Job:** convert the reaction into a small, specific, self-authored promise.

Immediately after the cost mirror, ask the customer to choose something. Days per week.
What they'd do with the time back. Which part they want to work on first. The content of
the choice matters less than the fact that they made it — this is commitment and
consistency, and Duolingo's daily-goal step is the canonical implementation.

Two conditions or it backfires:

- The choice must **change something real** in the product. A goal you collect and ignore
  is worse than no goal; customers notice.
- Offer the low option without penalty. "Two days a week" must be presented as a legitimate
  choice, not the loser option in a shame ladder.

**Failure mode:** collecting the commitment and then never referring to it again.

---

## 3. Commitment → habit

**Job:** get to a second and third session.

The customer is not yet a customer. Do not sell here. What earns the return visit:

- **A reason to come back that is theirs, not yours.** A number that updates. A streak
  belongs in this category *only* if missing a day is neutral. Streak-shame in a distress
  product converts a retention mechanic into an uninstall mechanic.
- **A single recommended action per session.** Not a menu. A person who is spending their
  day making anxious decisions about themselves does not want a sixth decision.
- **Visible near-term progress.** The gap between install and outcome is where people quit.
  Something has to move in week one even if the real change takes twelve.

**Failure mode:** a home screen that is a dashboard. Dashboards invite scrutiny of every
individual number, which in a distress product is the same cognitive move the product is
trying to interrupt.

---

## 4. Habit → the ask

**Job:** put the price next to the proof, once, at the right moment.

The right moment is the **second proof point** — the first time the customer sees their own
data confirm that something is changing. Not install. Not the end of onboarding. The
moment the number moves.

At that moment, three things are simultaneously true, and this is the whole design:

1. They have invested effort. Effort already spent is the strongest single predictor of
   paywall conversion (Noom's funnel is built entirely on this).
2. They have evidence the thing works *for them*, which no promise-based competitor has.
3. They are in a good mood about it, which means the ask is not landing on distress.

**What the paywall needs**, from the 2026 paywall literature:

- **Annual pre-selected**, badged, visually distinct. Health & Fitness takes 68% of revenue
  from annual and annual retains best.
- **Daily or monthly equivalent shown next to the annual figure.** Anchor low, bill high;
  "$44.99/yr · about $3.75 a month" beats either number alone.
- **A free-vs-paid comparison.** One of the most consistent additions across top apps,
  because a large share of people standing at a paywall still cannot say what they'd be
  buying. Write the free column honestly and generously; a visibly crippled free column
  reads as hostage-taking.
- **Trust content above the price**, not below. In this category trust *is* the conversion
  lever, and it usually means evidence, privacy, and refund clarity rather than testimonials.
- **A benefit-shaped CTA.** "Start my twelve weeks" over "Subscribe".
- **One plainly labelled dismiss, present in the first frame.**

**Failure mode:** the ask that arrives before the proof. It converts a fraction as well and
costs trust that never comes back.

---

## 5. The ask → trial

**Job:** long enough to change behaviour, loud enough that nobody is surprised.

Trials of 17–32 days convert at 42.5% versus 25.5% under four days. The mechanism is not
patience — it is that a longer trial spans enough sessions for the habit to form, and a
habit is what renews.

Set the trial to span **at least two full cycles of whatever the product's unit of progress
is.** For a weekly protocol that means fourteen days minimum.

The ethical requirement that comes with a long trial, and it is not optional:

- State the end date as a **date**, not a duration. "Free until 21 August" not "14 days free".
- Say plainly that a reminder will arrive before it ends, and then send it.
- Make cancelling take the same number of taps as subscribing, and say where the button is.

Do this and the long trial is a fair deal. Skip it and it is a subscription trap with a
better conversion rate and a worse business.

---

## 6. Trial → renewal

**Job:** survive the first renewal, then the second.

First-renewal churn runs 15–40% on monthly and ~30% of annual subscriptions are cancelled
inside the first month. Churn does not stabilise until roughly the second annual renewal.

What moves it:

- **Full price beats discount.** In annual Health & Fitness the discounted cohort churns
  worse than the full-price cohort. A discount buys a customer who was never going to stay.
- **Keep proving it.** The number that sold the subscription has to keep updating. A product
  that stops showing evidence after payment is a product people cancel at renewal.
- **Own the plateau.** In any behaviour-change product there is a stretch where progress
  flattens and people conclude it stopped working. Name it in the product *before* they hit
  it. An expected plateau is a stage; an unexpected one is a refund.
- **Monthly is not the enemy.** Monthly wins when trust is low or the use case is
  time-boxed. In a distress product, offering monthly is itself a trust signal: it says the
  product expects to earn the next month.

---

## 7. Lapse → winback

**Job:** make returning easy and unembarrassing.

The person who stopped using a mental health app usually stopped during a bad stretch. They
are carrying some shame about it. Every word of the winback either dissolves that or adds
to it.

- **Never** "we miss you", "you've fallen behind", "your progress is at risk".
- Do state a neutral fact and open a door: *"Your data is still here. Nothing was lost."*
- Restore their history intact and visibly. The record surviving the gap is the product's
  best argument.
- Preserve any lifetime bests across the gap. Reset the current counter, keep the record.
- Cap the reach-outs. Two, then stop. A product that keeps calling has become the anxiety.

---

## Hardship access

Not a funnel stage. A structural commitment, and it belongs in every product in this space.

Somebody who cannot pay is not a lower-value customer — in a health product they are often
the one with the most to gain. Offer a no-questions hardship tier: visible, not buried, no
form, no proof, no explanation required.

The commercial case is real, though it should not be the reason: the marginal cost is zero,
it converts word-of-mouth in exactly the communities where this kind of product spreads, and
it is the single clearest signal that the business is not built on desperation. The reason
to do it is that it is correct.

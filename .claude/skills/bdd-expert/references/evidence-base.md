# BDD — Evidence Base

Every figure below is attributed. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated rather than averaged away. Confidence is graded: **[Established]** = replicated across independent samples or confirmed by meta-analysis; **[Probable]** = consistent findings, limited replication; **[Contested/Unknown]** = thin, conflicting, or correlational-only evidence.

---

## 1. Prevalence

**[Established] General adult population: approximately 2%.**

Population surveys converge tightly:

| Study | Sample | Point prevalence |
|---|---|---|
| Rief et al. (2006), *Psychol Med*, German nationwide | n = 2,552 | 1.7% |
| Koran et al. (2008), *CNS Spectr*, US nationwide | n = 2,048 | 2.4% |
| Buhlmann et al. (2010), *Psychiatry Res* 178(1):171–5, German nationwide | n = 2,510 | 1.8% |

The 2024 *Nature Reviews Disease Primers* on BDD (Rück and colleagues, doi:10.1038/s41572-024-00577-z) states the headline figure as **~2% of adults**, underdiagnosed and undertreated.

Useful comparison for conveying scale: this is more common than anorexia nervosa or schizophrenia.

**[Established] Prevalence is dramatically higher in appearance-focused clinical settings.**

- Cosmetic surgery / dermatology settings: **~20%**
- Rhinoplasty specifically: **~20%**
- General psychiatric settings: **~7%**
- Plastic surgery patients: up to **24%** in some samples

Veale et al. (2016), *Body Image* 18:168–86 — "Body dysmorphic disorder in different settings: A systematic review and estimated weighted prevalence" — is the standard citation for the setting-by-setting breakdown.

**Caution on newer meta-analyses.** A 2023 meta-analysis (22 studies, n = 7,159) reported a pooled point prevalence of 11.3%, and at least one 2025 meta-analysis reports figures as high as 17% in "general population." These are dominated by clinical and student convenience samples using self-report screeners rather than diagnostic interviews, and screeners over-identify relative to clinician diagnosis. **Do not quote 11–17% as a general population rate.** The population-survey figure of ~2% is the defensible one; say so if someone cites the higher numbers.

## 2. Onset and course

**[Established] Onset is adolescent.** Bjornsson et al. (2013), *Compr Psychiatry* 54(7):893–903 — "Age at onset and clinical correlates in body dysmorphic disorder":

- **Mean age at onset: 16.7 years** (replicated across two independent samples)
- **~66–67% had onset before age 18**
- Subclinical symptoms typically precede full-criteria BDD by several years
- Early onset predicts: gradual (vs. acute) onset, more severe symptoms, **lifetime suicide attempt**, and greater comorbidity — including eating disorders, substance use disorders, anxiety disorders, and personality disorders (particularly borderline).

Clinical implication worth stating: adolescence is where this starts, which is exactly why appearance-rating products aimed at teenagers are not a neutral proposition.

**[Established] Untreated, the course is chronic.** Phillips et al. (2013), *Psychol Med* 43(5):1109–17 — 4-year prospective observational follow-up, n = 166 adults and adolescents:

- Cumulative probability over 4 years: **0.20 for full remission**, **0.55 for full or partial remission**
- At 1-year follow-up, **>90% had not been symptom-free for even 8 consecutive weeks**
- At 4 years, **>60% had been continuously symptomatic for the entire period**
- Poorer outcome predicted by: greater symptom severity at intake, longer lifetime duration, adult (vs. adolescent) status

The last predictor matters practically — earlier intervention has better odds.

Note a genuine tension in the literature: the Harvard/Brown Anxiety Research Project (HARP) 8-year data reported a much more optimistic 76% probability of full recovery with only 14% recurrence — but that sample was ascertained for anxiety disorders with BDD as a comorbidity, not for primary BDD, and remission often took over 5 years. The pessimistic figures come from samples with primary BDD. Report both if the course question is asked seriously.

## 3. Suicidality

**[Established] This is the reason BDD is not a cosmetic concern.** Phillips & Menard (2006), *Am J Psychiatry* 163(7):1280–2 — first prospective study of suicidality in BDD, n = 185, up to 4 years:

- **Suicidal ideation: mean 57.8% of subjects per year**
- **Suicide attempts: mean 2.6% per year**
- **Completed suicide: 0.3% per year** (2 subjects) — the authors describe this rate as markedly elevated; it is roughly an order of magnitude above general-population rates, though the small number of events makes the estimate preliminary.

Higher risk subgroups: **muscle dysmorphia** (higher suicidality, higher substance use, poorer quality of life than other BDD presentations — noted in DSM-5-TR), and **early onset**.

## 4. Comorbidity

**[Established]** Phillips et al. (1994), n = 100, lifetime rates:
- Major depressive disorder: **80%**
- Social phobia: **37%**
- OCD: **34%**

Gunstad & Phillips (2003), *Compr Psychiatry* 44(4):270–6 — "Axis I comorbidity in body dysmorphic disorder," n = 293. Key finding is that comorbidity is usually *stacked*, not singular:
- Social anxiety disorder **alone**: only 2%
- Social anxiety + depression: **32%**
- Social anxiety + depression + OCD: **14%**
- OCD + major depression: **25%**

**[Established] Temporal ordering:** social phobia typically *precedes* BDD onset; depression and substance use disorders typically *follow* it. This supports treating depression in BDD as substantially secondary — a consequence of the appearance preoccupation and the isolation it produces, rather than an independent process.

## 5. Aetiology

### Genetics

**[Probable] Heritability of dysmorphic concern ≈ 44%.** Monzani et al. (2012), twin study using the St Thomas UK Adult Twin Registry, n = 3,544 female twins: genetic factors accounted for ~44% of variance in dysmorphic concerns; non-shared environment plus measurement error accounted for the remaining ~56%; **shared environmental effects were negligible**.

Monzani et al. (2012), *Am J Med Genet B* also found **genetic overlap between body dysmorphic concerns and obsessive-compulsive symptoms** — molecular support for the DSM-5 decision to reclassify BDD into the Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders chapter.

Caveat to state: the sample was female-only and used a dimensional self-report of dysmorphic concern, not diagnosed BDD. Generalization to clinical BDD in men is an inference, not a finding.

### Childhood adversity

**[Established] Rates of childhood maltreatment are high.** Didie et al. (2006), *Child Abuse Negl* 30(10):1105–15:

- **78.7%** reported a history of childhood maltreatment of any kind
- Emotional neglect: **68.0%**
- Emotional abuse: **56.0%**
- Physical abuse: **34.7%**
- Physical neglect: **33.3%**
- Sexual abuse: **28.0%**

**[Established] Appearance-related teasing is among the strongest associated adverse experiences.** Meta-analytic association for experienced teasing r ≈ 0.42. BDD samples report more appearance- and competency-related teasing than healthy controls, and perceived teasing severity tracks BDD symptom severity.

**Critical caveat, always state it:** these are *retrospective self-reports in clinical samples*. They establish association, not causation, and recall of childhood adversity is itself affected by current mood state. Plenty of people with severe BDD report unremarkable childhoods. Never tell someone their BDD was caused by their upbringing.

### Neurobiology and visual processing

**[Probable] A bias toward local/detail processing over global/holistic processing.** The programme of work here is Feusner and colleagues (UCLA):

- Feusner et al. (2007), *Arch Gen Psychiatry* — faces: **left-hemisphere hyperactivity** in higher-order visual processing regions relative to controls, consistent with predominantly detailed and analytic processing.
- Feusner et al. (2011), *Psychol Med* — "Abnormalities of object visual processing in BDD": the same abnormality appears for **houses**, not just faces. This is the important one — it indicates a general visual-processing difference rather than a face- or self-specific one.
- Feusner et al. (2010), *Arch Gen Psychiatry* — **hypoactivity in visual cortical systems for low spatial frequency information** (which carries holistic/configural content), alongside **hyperactivity in frontostriatal systems** for unaltered images.
- Symptom severity correlates inversely with activity in holistic-processing regions.

**What this licenses you to say:** there is measurable, replicated evidence that attention and visual processing in BDD skew toward fine detail at the expense of the whole. This is the mechanistic basis for perceptual retraining, and it is the honest answer to "am I imagining it or do I actually see something different?" — *something in how you're processing the image is genuinely different; what it isn't is a more accurate view.*

**What this does not license:** claiming BDD is "caused by" a visual-processing defect. Direction of causation is unresolved; the abnormalities may be consequences of years of detail-focused scrutiny rather than causes. Say so.

Behavioural convergence: Buhlmann et al. — **BDD participants rate their own attractiveness significantly lower than independent evaluators rate them**, while rating others' attractive faces *higher* than controls do. The distortion is specific and self-directed, not a general perceptual deficit. Note also a 2024 finding (*Body Image*) that BDD participants show no superior facial-symmetry discrimination, and that *perceived* — not objective — symmetry predicts perceived attractiveness. The "BDD patients have superior aesthetic sensitivity" hypothesis is **[Contested]** and not well supported.

## 6. Mirror gazing — the maintaining behaviour, measured

**[Established]** Veale & Riley (2001), *Behav Res Ther* 39(12):1381–93 — "Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the ugliest of them all?", 52 BDD patients vs. 55 controls:

- ~**80%** of people with BDD engage in mirror gazing
- Both more frequent short sessions (~5 min) **and** markedly longer sessions — **up to 170 minutes** in a single session
- People with BDD were more likely to focus on an **internal impression or felt sense** rather than the external reflection, and on **specific parts** rather than the whole
- **Distress increased significantly after long mirror sessions** (~18.5% increase)

This is the empirical backbone of the psychoeducation. Checking does not reduce distress — it measurably increases it. The study was prompted by a patient who reported spending six hours in front of a mirror.

Related **[Established]** finding: appearance comparison is elevated in BDD relative to controls in both frequency and distress, and is directed at appearance-relevant targets.

## 7. Cosmetic procedures — outcomes

**[Established] Cosmetic treatment overwhelmingly fails to resolve BDD.** Crerand, Menard & Phillips (2010), *Ann Plast Surg* — surgical and minimally invasive procedures in people with BDD:

- **Only 2.3% of procedures produced long-term improvement in overall BDD symptoms**
- ~**25%** produced longer-term improvement in preoccupation with *the specific treated feature* — the concern very commonly migrates to another body area
- The modal outcome was **no change** in BDD severity; a minority worsened

Phillips et al. (2001) and Crerand et al. (2005) report that a large majority of people with BDD seek, and most receive, cosmetic treatment. Veale's work additionally documents **"DIY surgery"** — patients attempting to alter their own appearance surgically, sometimes with severe injury. Treat any mention of this as a medical emergency.

Contrast to make explicit: people *without* BDD generally do report improved body image after cosmetic procedures. The failure is specific to BDD, which is why screening in cosmetic settings matters.

## 8. Treatment access and recognition

**[Established] The condition is systematically missed.**

- **Only ~15.2%** of individuals with BDD had ever been *diagnosed* with BDD
- Lifetime rates of any mental health treatment: **~39.9%**
- Principal barriers: **shame**, low perceived need, and preference for cosmetic/medical over psychiatric treatment
- Patients rarely volunteer symptoms — roughly **one fifth said they would disclose appearance concerns only if directly asked**
- Many do not construe the problem as psychiatric at all

Practical consequence: **BDD must be screened for with direct questions.** It will not surface on its own. See `assessment.md`.

## 9. Sex and gender differences

**[Established]** Phillips, Menard & Fay (2006), *Compr Psychiatry* 47(2):77–87, n = 200; and Phillips & Diaz (1997):

- **Skin is the most common concern in both men and women.**
- **Men** more often: genitals, body build/muscularity, thinning hair/balding.
- **Women** more often: skin, stomach, weight, breasts/chest, buttocks, thighs, legs, hips, excess body/facial hair; and concern with **more body areas overall**.
- Women performed more repetitive/safety behaviours — camouflaging, mirror checking, changing clothes, skin picking — and had higher rates of comorbid eating disorders.
- Some studies find poorer insight in women; this is less consistently replicated.

Framing note: overall **similarities outweigh differences**. BDD is not a "female disorder" — prevalence is roughly comparable across sexes, and muscle dysmorphia is overwhelmingly male.

### Muscle dysmorphia

A DSM-5 **specifier**, not a separate diagnosis: preoccupation that one's build is too small or insufficiently muscular. Apply the specifier even when other body areas are also a concern.

- Predominantly male. Prevalence estimates: ~2.2% of adolescent boys and 1.4% of adolescent girls (Australian data); ~5.9% in college students; **13.6–44% among male weightlifters** (wide range reflects heterogeneous criteria and samples — quote the range, not a point estimate).
- Strongly associated with **anabolic-androgenic steroid (AAS) use**. Global lifetime AAS prevalence ~6.4% in men, ~1.6% in women; approximately **one third of AAS users develop dependence**, with withdrawal featuring depression, anxiety, fatigue, and sexual dysfunction.
- DSM-5-TR: muscle dysmorphia presentations have **higher rates of suicidality and substance use disorders, and poorer quality of life** than other BDD forms.

## 10. Social media

**[Contested] — be scrupulously careful here; this is where confident overclaiming is most common.**

What the evidence actually supports:

- **[Established]** Experimental exposure to appearance-ideal social media images produces a **moderate negative effect on state body image**. Taking and posting a selfie lowers self-rated attractiveness in experimental conditions.
- **[Probable]** Frequency of use of **image-based** platforms correlates positively with body dysmorphic symptoms; appearance-based *motivation* for use is independently associated with symptoms — arguably more predictive than raw screen time.
- **[Contested]** Longitudinal studies show a **small but statistically significant** negative association between social media use and body image over time. Small. The gap between this and the popular claim that social media *causes* an epidemic of body dysmorphia is substantial.

What is **not** established: that social media causes clinical BDD. BDD was described in the 1890s (Morselli's *dysmorphophobia*) and its prevalence and adolescent onset age were documented well before image-based platforms existed. Directionality is genuinely unresolved — appearance-preoccupied people preferentially seek image-based platforms.

Defensible summary: *"Image-based social media measurably worsens state body image and correlates with dysmorphic symptoms, particularly when used for appearance-related motives. Whether it causes the disorder is unresolved, and the longitudinal effect sizes are small. It is more plausibly an accelerant than an origin."*

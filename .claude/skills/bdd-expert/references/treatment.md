# Treatment — What Works, How It Works, and What Doesn't

Two first-line treatments have randomized support: **CBT tailored specifically to BDD** and **SSRIs**. Generic supportive counselling and generic CBT are not adequate substitutes.

---

## 1. CBT for BDD

### The evidence

**Meta-analysis — the headline citation.** Harrison A, Fernández de la Cruz L, Enander J, Radua J, Mataix-Cols D (2016). Cognitive-behavioral therapy for body dysmorphic disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *Clinical Psychology Review* 48:43–51.

- 7 RCTs, **N = 299**
- CBT vs. waitlist or credible psychological placebo:
  - **BDD symptoms: d = −1.22** (large)
  - Depressive symptoms: d = −0.49
  - Insight / delusionality: d = −0.56
- Gains maintained at 2–4 month follow-up (3 studies; δ = −0.89, 95% CI −1.24 to −0.54)

Note the insight finding: CBT improves *delusionality*. Poor insight is not a contraindication.

**Key individual trials:**

- **Wilhelm S, et al. (2014).** Modular cognitive-behavioral therapy for body dysmorphic disorder: a randomized controlled trial. *Behavior Therapy* 45(3):314–327. 22 weekly sessions vs. waitlist. Roughly 50% of the treatment group achieved ≥30% BDD-YBOCS improvement at 12 weeks vs. 12% of waitlist; by end of the 22-week study ~81% of all treated participants were responders. This is the trial behind the CBT-BDD manual.
- **Wilhelm S, et al. (2019).** Efficacy and posttreatment effects of therapist-delivered CBT vs supportive psychotherapy for adults with BDD: a randomized clinical trial. *JAMA Psychiatry*. The important comparison — supportive psychotherapy is what most people with BDD *actually receive*. CBT-BDD outperformed it.
- **Veale D, et al. (2014).** *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* 83(6):341–353. CBT vs. anxiety management, Maudsley Hospital. CBT superior — i.e. the specific BDD content matters, not just therapeutic contact plus relaxation.
- **Veale D, et al. (1996).** *Behaviour Research and Therapy*. The original pilot RCT (n=19) and cognitive-behavioural model.
- **Mataix-Cols D, et al. (2015).** *J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry*. First RCT of CBT in **adolescents** with BDD, n = 30, 14 sessions (~15 hours), including psychoeducation, goal setting, ERP, and relapse prevention. Long-term follow-up showed maintained gains.

**Digital delivery — genuinely promising, worth knowing:**
- Wilhelm et al. (2022), *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* 91(4):277 — app-based CBT for BDD with coach support, RCT. Preceded by a 12-week open trial with a very large within-group effect (d = 2.60).
- Enander, Mataix-Cols and colleagues — therapist-guided internet-delivered CBT (BDD-NET) for adults and adolescents, with feasibility and RCT support and durable gains.
- A 2025 RCT of internet-based therapist-guided CBT for adolescents and young adults (n = 45, ages 15–21, 12 sessions) vs. supportive online therapy showed efficacy against an *active* control.

This matters for access: BDD-specialist therapists are scarce, and guided digital CBT has real evidence behind it. It is not a substitute for care in high-risk cases.

### The components — what CBT-BDD actually consists of

The reference manual is **Wilhelm, Phillips & Steketee (2013), *Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Body Dysmorphic Disorder: A Treatment Manual*** (Guilford). Modular design: core modules for everyone, optional modules for specific presentations.

**Core modules:**

1. **Psychoeducation and individualized model.** Build the person's own maintaining cycle: trigger → appearance thought → distress → checking/avoidance/reassurance → brief relief → strengthened urge. Most of the therapeutic movement in the first sessions comes from the person recognizing they are inside a *known mechanism*.

2. **Cognitive restructuring.** Identify appearance-related automatic thoughts and the distortions carrying them (all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading, catastrophizing, emotional reasoning, mental filter, labeling, fortune telling). Then move deeper to **core beliefs** — typically about worth, acceptability, and lovability being contingent on appearance. The target is not "my nose is fine," it is "my nose is not the arbiter of whether I'm acceptable."

3. **Exposure and response prevention (ERP).** *Exposure*: enter avoided situations (uncovered, unedited, in daylight, in social settings). *Response prevention*: withhold the compulsion — no mirror check, no camouflage adjustment, no reassurance request, no comparison scroll. Graded via a hierarchy. Best done as **behavioural experiments** with an explicit prediction ("people will stare and comment") tested against the observed outcome, rather than as pure habituation.

4. **Perceptual (mirror) retraining.** The distinctive BDD component and the one most often omitted by non-specialists. The person learns to view themselves at conversational distance, for a set duration, describing their whole appearance **neutrally and non-judgmentally** ("hair, dark, shoulder length") rather than evaluatively ("gross, uneven"), covering the whole body rather than zooming in on the disliked feature. Grounded in the local/global processing findings (`evidence-base.md` §5). Extended outward: practising broad attention in situations where the person selectively attends to their own or others' appearance.

5. **Attention retraining.** Shifting from self-focused to externally focused attention in social settings — the same principle as in social anxiety treatment.

6. **Relapse prevention.** Explicit plan for lapses, booster sessions, continued self-directed exposure.

**Optional modules** targeted to presentation: skin picking / hair pulling habit reversal; **cosmetic surgery seeking**; muscle dysmorphia and associated behaviours (steroid use, compulsive exercise, dietary rigidity); depression; weight and shape concerns.

### Adjuncts with some support

- **Imagery rescripting** for BDD-related early aversive memories (teasing, bullying) — single case series evidence, promising, not RCT-established.
- **ACT / body image flexibility** approaches — reasonable theoretical fit, thinner evidence base than CBT for BDD specifically.
- **Mindfulness-based CBT** — pilot RCT support, web-delivered format.
- **Metacognitive therapy** — consecutive case series only.

Represent these honestly as adjuncts or emerging, not as first-line.

## 2. Pharmacotherapy

### The evidence

**SSRIs are the medication class with evidence. This holds for delusional BDD too.**

**Fluoxetine RCT.** Phillips KA, et al. (2002). A randomized placebo-controlled trial of fluoxetine in body dysmorphic disorder. *Archives of General Psychiatry* 59(4):381–388.
- 67 randomized, 12 weeks double-blind after 1 week single-blind placebo
- **Response: 53% (18/34) fluoxetine vs. 18% (6/33) placebo**, p = .003
- Separation from placebo began at **week 8** and continued through weeks 10 and 12 — this late separation is the clinically important detail
- **Delusional and non-delusional patients responded equally well**

**Escitalopram relapse prevention.** Phillips KA, et al. (2016). Pharmacotherapy relapse prevention in body dysmorphic disorder: a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. *American Journal of Psychiatry* 173(9):887–895.
- Phase 1: open-label escitalopram, 14 weeks, N = 100 → **67.0% of treated (81.1% of completers) responded**
- Phase 2: 58 responders randomized to continue escitalopram vs. switch to placebo for 6 months
- **Relapse: 18% on escitalopram vs. 40% on placebo**
- Among continuers, **35.7% improved further** during continuation

Two conclusions to carry: responders keep improving past the initial response, and **discontinuation roughly doubles relapse risk**. Continuation treatment is not optional padding.

**Augmentation.** Phillips (2005), *Am J Psychiatry* 162(2):377–379 — placebo-controlled **pimozide augmentation** of fluoxetine was **not** superior to placebo, including in delusional patients. Antipsychotic augmentation is not supported by the controlled evidence; buspirone augmentation has open-label support only.

### Dosing — the part clinicians most often get wrong

**BDD generally requires higher SSRI doses and longer trials than most other indications.**

- Adequate trial: **12–16 weeks**, including **at least 3–4 weeks at maximum tolerated dose**
- Target doses cited in the BDD literature: fluoxetine **80 mg/d**; escitalopram **30 mg/d**; sertraline **200 mg/d** (up to 400 mg/d in some patients); fluvoxamine **300 mg/d**; paroxetine **60 mg/d**
- Titrate gradually (e.g. sertraline in 25–50 mg increments every 2–3 weeks)

Doses above standard licensed maxima are used in this literature and require prescriber judgement and monitoring. **Always frame this as information about what the evidence shows, addressed to the prescriber's decision — never as a dosing instruction to an individual.**

The common failure mode: a patient is tried on a standard antidepressant dose for 6 weeks, doesn't respond, and is told medication doesn't work for them. Given that fluoxetine did not separate from placebo until week 8, that trial was simply too short and too low.

### Guidelines

**NICE CG31** (OCD and BDD, UK). Scope: adults, young people, and children **aged 8 and over** — stepped care.

*Adults:*
- **Moderate** functional impairment: choice of an **SSRI** or more intensive **individual CBT including ERP** addressing BDD-specific features
- **Severe** impairment: combined SSRI + CBT

*Children and young people* — for both age bands the first-line psychological treatment is the same: **CBT including ERP, involving the family or carers**. The bands differ only in how strongly medication is recommended when that isn't enough, and both require multidisciplinary review first:

| Age band | If inadequate response to CBT+ERP with family/carers |
|---|---|
| **8–11** | Addition of an SSRI **may be considered** |
| **12–18** | Addition of an SSRI **should be offered**, with careful monitoring especially early in treatment |

Two details worth carrying precisely, because both are easy to get subtly wrong:

- **The age band matters when you're advising about a specific young person.** A 16-year-old falls in the 12–18 band, where SSRI augmentation is a *should be offered* rather than the weaker *may be considered*. Don't collapse the two bands into a generic "for under-18s" — the strength of the recommendation genuinely differs, and 8–11 is the more conservative one.
- **Where fluoxetine belongs.** NICE specifies that when an SSRI is prescribed to *children and young people* with BDD, **fluoxetine** should be the one used. This is a paediatric recommendation. It does **not** mean NICE names fluoxetine as the SSRI of choice for BDD in general — that over-generalization is a real and observed failure mode, and it is prescribing-relevant, so state the age scope whenever you cite it.

If further treatment is unsuccessful or not tolerated, CG31 allows considering a different SSRI or clomipramine with careful monitoring, still combined with CBT including ERP.

Also relevant: the International College of Obsessive-Compulsive Spectrum Disorders / ECNP OCRD Network **treatment synthesis and consensus** on BDD.

## 3. What does not work

**Cosmetic and dermatological procedures.** The most important negative finding in the field. Crerand, Menard & Phillips (2010): **only 2.3% of surgical and minimally invasive procedures produced long-term improvement in overall BDD symptoms.** ~25% improved preoccupation with the specific treated feature, but concern commonly migrates elsewhere. Modal outcome: no change. Some worsen. People *without* BDD generally do benefit from cosmetic procedures — the failure is specific to BDD.

**Reassurance.** Provides minutes of relief, strengthens the compulsion, and transfers authority over the person's body to an external judge. This applies to family, friends, clinicians, and chatbots equally. See the top of `SKILL.md`.

**Antipsychotic monotherapy for delusional BDD.** Not supported. Delusional BDD is BDD, and it responds to SSRIs.

**Generic supportive therapy.** Better than nothing; inferior to CBT-BDD in head-to-head RCT (Wilhelm 2019). Someone who "tried therapy and it didn't work" very often had non-specific therapy, not CBT-BDD. This is worth surfacing — it changes what they do next.

**Appearance self-monitoring in any form** — daily rating, tracking, scoring, photo comparison, progress rating. This *is* the maintaining behaviour. Building it into a product does not make it therapeutic.

## 4. Prognosis — what to tell someone honestly

Untreated, BDD is chronic: 4-year full remission probability 0.20, full-or-partial 0.55 (Phillips 2013). Treated with CBT-BDD, the meta-analytic effect against control is large (d = −1.22), with roughly half responding by 12 weeks and around 80% by 22 weeks in the Wilhelm 2014 trial. SSRIs produce response in roughly half to two thirds, and continuation halves relapse risk.

The honest framing: **this is one of the more treatable serious psychiatric conditions, and one of the least treated** — ~15% ever diagnosed, ~40% ever receiving any mental health treatment. The gap is recognition and access, not the absence of effective treatment. That is a genuinely hopeful message and it does not require overselling anything.
